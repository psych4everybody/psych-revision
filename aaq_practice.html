1. What is the AAQ?
FeatureDetail
Full nameArticle Analysis Question. Question 1 of the free-response section.
Marks7 in total.
Time25 minutes: a 10 minute reading period, then 15 minutes of writing.
Skills testedResearch methods (Science Practice 2), data interpretation (Practice 3) and argumentation (Practice 4).
What you readOne summarised peer-reviewed study of about 300 to 400 words, with an Introduction (context and hypothesis), Participants, Method, and Results and Discussion.
What you writeSix short answers, Parts A to F. This is not an essay. Write clear, evidence-linked sentences for each part.
2. The six parts and how marks are earned
PartWhat it asksWhat earns the mark
A Research method (1)Identify the method used.Name it clearly (experiment, correlational study, survey, case study, naturalistic observation). A short reason from the study helps.
B Operational definition (1)State how a variable was measured in this study.A concrete, measurable procedure: a score, a count, a rating, a time. Not a general definition of the concept.
C Basic statistics (1)Interpret a statistic from the results.Use the actual number (a mean, a difference between means, r, a percentage) and explain what it means for the study's findings.
D Ethics (1)Identify an ethical guideline and describe how it was used.Name a guideline that the study actually describes (such as informed consent, confidentiality, protection from harm or debriefing) AND describe how the researchers applied it.
E Generalizability (1)Explain how far the findings apply to a wider population.Make a claim about a population AND support it with evidence about the participants (age, background, setting, how they were recruited). Sample size alone does not earn the mark.
F Support or refute (2)Explain how a finding supports or refutes the hypothesis.1 mark for using a specific result, or for explaining support without a result. 2 marks for a specific result AND an accurate explanation of how it supports or refutes the hypothesis.
3. Using the 10 minute reading period

Read the six questions first. Then read the study and mark what is relevant to each part.

PartWhat to look for
A Method"Randomly assigned," "manipulated," "experimental and control groups" (experiment). "Measured the relationship between" (correlational). "Studied one individual" (case study). "Observed in a natural setting" (naturalistic observation). "Completed a questionnaire" (survey).
B Operational definitionExactly how the variable was measured. Numbers, scales, tasks, instruments. For example: aggression was measured as the number of times participants pressed a button.
C StatisticsAny numbers in the Results section: means, percentages, correlation coefficients, p-values. Underline them.
D EthicsMentions of consent, debriefing, confidentiality, possible harm or ethics approval.
E GeneralizabilityWho the participants are, where they came from, and how they were recruited. Do they represent a narrow group?
F Support or refuteThe hypothesis in the Introduction first. Then the key results. Does the data match the prediction?

Suggested pacing for the 15 writing minutes: A 1 minute, B 2, C 2, D 2, E 3, F 4, and 1 minute to check. This is a guide, not a rule.

4. Worked example with model answers

This practice study was adapted from classroom revision materials. It is not an official College Board item.

Research summary: The effect of background music on concentration during problem-solving

Introduction. Researchers have long been interested in whether the acoustic environment affects cognitive performance. Some studies suggest that background music can improve mood and productivity, while others argue that music, particularly music with lyrics, adds a second auditory stream that competes for attention. Dr. Ava Sharma and Dr. Ben Carter tested whether the type of background music affects concentration during a problem-solving task. They hypothesised that participants listening to instrumental music would show higher concentration than those listening to music with lyrics, and that both music groups would show lower concentration than participants working in silence.

Participants. Sixty undergraduate students (mean age = 20.3 years, SD = 1.4) were recruited through posted flyers on a single university campus. All participants reported normal hearing. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before any data collection. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions: instrumental music (n = 20), music with lyrics (n = 20), or silence (n = 20).

Method. All participants completed a standardised set of 30 logic puzzles in a laboratory room under one of three sound conditions. Group A (Instrumental) listened to a pre-selected playlist of classical instrumental music at 55 decibels through noise-cancelling headphones. Group B (Lyrics) listened to a pre-selected playlist of popular music with prominent vocals at 55 decibels through the same headphones. Group C (Silence) wore the same headphones with no audio. Concentration was operationally defined as the number of logic puzzles answered correctly within a 20-minute period. All participants were debriefed immediately after the session and told the study's true purpose.

Results and Discussion. The silence group correctly solved the highest number of puzzles (mean = 22.4, SD = 3.1). The instrumental music group performed similarly (mean = 21.8, SD = 3.4). The music-with-lyrics group showed notably lower performance (mean = 15.6, SD = 4.7). A comparison of the three groups revealed a statistically significant difference between conditions (p = 0.003). The researchers concluded that music with lyrics impairs concentration during complex cognitive tasks, while instrumental music does not significantly affect performance relative to silence. These results partially support the researchers' original hypothesis.

Part A: Research method (1 mark)

This study used the experimental research method. I know it is an experiment because the researchers randomly assigned participants to one of three conditions (instrumental music, music with lyrics, or silence) and directly manipulated the type of auditory environment (the independent variable) while measuring puzzle performance (the dependent variable).

Name the method clearly. A reason from the study shows that you understand why it qualifies. Clues: "randomly assigned," three conditions, and a manipulated environment.

Part B: Operational definition (1 mark)

In this study, concentration was operationally defined as the number of logic puzzles answered correctly within a 20-minute period. This gives concentration a precise, countable and replicable measurement that can be compared across groups.

Do not write "the ability to focus." That is a definition of the concept. The operational definition is how this study measured it: puzzles solved correctly in 20 minutes.

Part C: Basic statistics (1 mark)

The instrumental music group solved an average of 21.8 puzzles correctly, while the music-with-lyrics group solved an average of 15.6 puzzles correctly, a difference of 6.2 puzzles. This indicates that participants who listened to music with lyrics performed substantially worse on the problem-solving task than those who listened to instrumental music. It suggests that lyrics specifically, not music in general, interfere with concentration during complex thinking.

Use the numbers and explain what the difference means. Listing the means without interpreting them would not earn the mark.

Part D: Ethical guideline (1 mark)

The researchers applied the ethical guideline of informed consent. According to the summary, written informed consent was obtained from all participants before any data collection began, so participants agreed to take part voluntarily and knew what the study involved. The researchers also applied debriefing: participants were told the study's true purpose immediately after their session.

Either guideline earns the mark. Name it and connect it to evidence from the summary.

Part E: Generalizability (1 mark)

The findings have limited generalizability beyond the sample studied. All participants were undergraduate students recruited from a single university campus through posted flyers, which is a convenience sample from a narrow group. This sample does not represent the wider adult population, which varies in age, education and musical background. The findings may not apply to older adults, people outside university, or people with different levels of musical training.

Make a claim about a population and back it with participant evidence: who they were, where they came from and how they were recruited. Saying only "the sample is small" does not earn the mark.

Part F: Support or refute the hypothesis (2 marks)

The findings partially support the researchers' hypothesis. The researchers predicted that instrumental music would produce higher concentration than music with lyrics, and that both music groups would show lower concentration than the silence group. The first prediction was clearly supported: the music-with-lyrics group (mean = 15.6) performed much lower than the instrumental music group (mean = 21.8), which suggests that lyrics disrupt concentration. The second prediction was only partly supported: the silence group (mean = 22.4) did outperform the lyrics group, but the difference between silence (22.4) and instrumental music (21.8) was very small. This suggests that instrumental music does not meaningfully reduce concentration compared with silence, which goes against the hypothesis.

1 mark for a specific result from the study (the actual means). 1 mark for an accurate explanation of why that result supports or challenges the prediction. A result with no explanation, or an explanation with no result, earns 1 mark at most.

5. Common AAQ mistakes and what to do instead
MistakeWhat to do instead
Defining a concept instead of operationalising it (Part B)Part B asks how the variable was measured in this study. "Concentration is the ability to focus" is a definition. "Concentration was measured as the number of puzzles solved correctly in 20 minutes" is an operational definition.
Repeating a number without interpreting it (Part C)State the number, then say what it means for the study: the direction, the size, and what it suggests about the variables.
Naming an ethical guideline the study never mentions (Part D)Use only the guideline the summary describes, and describe how the researchers applied it.
Claiming generalizability without participant evidence (Part E)"The findings may not generalize" is not enough. Use evidence about the participants, such as age, country, setting, or how they were recruited. Sample size alone does not earn the mark.
Writing "the results support the hypothesis" with no data (Part F)Quote a specific result (a mean, a percentage, an r value) and explain why it supports or refutes the prediction. You need both for 2 marks.
Claiming cause from a correlation (Parts C and F)A correlation coefficient shows a relationship, not cause and effect. Only an experiment can show that one variable causes a change in another.
Writing bullet pointsWrite complete sentences. Sentences show the reasoning that earns the explanation marks.
6. The Evidence-Based Question (EBQ) at a glance

EBQ practice will be added to this site later. Here is what to expect.

FeatureDetail
Marks and time7 marks. 45 minutes: a 15 minute reading period and 30 minutes of writing.
What you readThree summarised studies on a common topic. You use only two of them.
What you writeFive short answers: Part A (claim), B(i) and B(ii), C(i) and C(ii). Not one long essay.
PartWhat earns the marks
A Claim (1)One specific, defensible claim that answers the question. It must take a position.
B(i) Evidence (1)One specific piece of evidence from one source, cited (for example "Source B").
B(ii) Explanation (2)Explain how the evidence supports your claim (1), and apply a named psychological concept from the course (1).
C(i) Evidence (1)Specific, cited evidence from a different source than B(i).
C(ii) Explanation (2)Explain how the evidence supports your claim (1), and apply a different named psychological concept from the course (1).

Most students lose marks on B(ii) and C(ii) by explaining the evidence in plain language without naming a psychological concept. Always apply a named concept to the specific evidence, and do not write textbook definitions on their own.
