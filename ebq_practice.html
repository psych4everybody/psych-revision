EBQ Revision Guide

Everything you need before you practise: what the question is, how marks are earned, how to use the reading period, and how to write each part. This page works offline and is closed while a practice is running.

1. What is the EBQ?
FeatureDetail
Full nameEvidence-Based Question. Question 2 of the free-response section.
Marks7 in total.
Time45 minutes: a 15 minute reading period, then 30 minutes of writing.
Skills testedConcept application (Science Practice 1) and argumentation (Practice 4). The EBQ is the only question on the exam that tests argumentation.
What you readThree short research summaries on one topic, and one question. You use only two of the three sources in your answer.
What you writeFive short answers: Part A, B(i), B(ii), C(i) and C(ii). This is not an essay. Each part is its own short answer in full sentences.
2. The five parts and how marks are earned
PartWhat it asksWhat earns the mark
A Claim (1)Make a claim that answers the question.One specific sentence that takes a position, for example a clear effect or a recommendation. Do not just repeat the question or describe a study.
B(i) Evidence (1)Give specific evidence from one source.A real result from the source (a number, a percentage, a difference or a clear pattern), with the source named (for example "Source B"). It must support your claim.
B(ii) Explanation (2)Explain how the evidence supports the claim, using a psychological concept.1 mark for explaining how the evidence supports the claim. 1 mark for applying a named AP Psychology concept that is not named in the sources, and applying it to the evidence.
C(i) Evidence (1)Give specific evidence from a different source.The same standard as B(i), from a different source.
C(ii) Explanation (2)Explain how this evidence supports the claim, using a different concept.The same standard as B(ii). The concept must be different from the one in B(ii) and must also not be named in the sources.

Where the points come from: you can earn 5 of the 7 marks by reading carefully, making a sensible claim and quoting specific evidence from two sources. The last 2 marks need you to name and apply a course concept correctly.

3. Using the 15 minute reading period
  1. Read the question and the five parts first.
  2. Skim all three sources. For each one, underline the main result and the numbers.
  3. Decide your claim. It should answer the question and be supported by at least two sources.
  4. Pick the two sources with the clearest evidence for your claim. You do not have to use the third.
  5. Next to each chosen result, note one or two course concepts that could explain it. Check that those concepts are not named in the sources.

Suggested pacing for the 30 writing minutes: A 3 minutes, B(i) 4, B(ii) 8, C(i) 4, C(ii) 8, and 3 minutes to check. This is a guide, not a rule.

4. How to write each part
PartSentence frame (fill in your own words)
A[Variable X] [increases / decreases / is linked to] [variable Y] for [the group or situation]. Take a position. Keep it to one sentence.
B(i)In Source [letter], [group] scored [number] compared with [number] for [group]. Use a result that is really in the source.
B(ii)This supports my claim because [what the result shows]. This is an example of [named course concept], because [how the concept fits this result]. Apply the concept. Do not just define it.
C(i)In Source [a different letter], [result].
C(ii)This also supports my claim because [what the result shows]. This relates to [a different named course concept], because [how it fits].
5. Which concepts count? (the most common way to lose marks)
  • The concept must come from the AP Psychology course, not from the sources. If a source already uses a term (for example "working memory" or "correlation coefficient"), that term cannot earn the concept mark.
  • The concept must fit the evidence you chose. Naming a concept that is only loosely related does not earn the mark.
  • B(ii) and C(ii) need two different concepts.
  • After you lock your answers, the review page lists the concepts that were named in the sources and some course concepts you could have used.
6. Common EBQ mistakes and what to do instead
MistakeWhat to do instead
A claim that only repeats the question (Part A)Say what the effect is, in which direction, and for whom. A claim that takes a position can be defended with evidence.
Evidence with no source named, or evidence that is vague (B(i), C(i))Name the source and quote a specific result. "Source B showed it helps" is too vague.
Using the same source twice (C(i))C(i) must come from a different source than B(i).
Explaining in plain language only (B(ii), C(ii))Name a course concept and show how it explains this specific evidence.
Using a concept the source already namesChoose a different concept from the course. The source's own terms cannot earn the concept mark.
Repeating the same concept in B(ii) and C(ii)Use two different concepts.
Claiming cause from a correlationA correlation shows a relationship, not cause and effect. Match the strength of your claim to the design of the study.
Writing one long essayWrite five separate short answers, each labelled with its part.
